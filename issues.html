Who Makes Foreign Policy?

The Constitution splits power over foreign policy and war between the President and Congress. The founders did this on purpose so that no single person could take the country to war.

The President (Article II)Congress (Article I)
Commander in Chief of the armed forces2Power to declare war1
Negotiates treatiesSenate must approve treaties by a two-thirds vote2
Appoints ambassadors and the Secretary of State and Secretary of DefenseSenate confirms those appointments2
Directs the military once force is authorizedRaises and funds the military ("power of the purse") and holds oversight hearings1

The War Powers Resolution (1973)

After Vietnam, Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over President Nixon's veto. It says the President must notify Congress within 48 hours of sending troops into hostilities, and must remove them within 60 days (plus 30 days to withdraw) unless Congress authorizes the action.3

In practice, presidents of both parties have found ways around it. Congress has also passed broad "Authorizations for Use of Military Force" (AUMFs). The 2001 AUMF, passed right after 9/11, is still in force 25 years later and has been used to justify operations in many countries.22, 26

The Iran Conflict (2026)

The biggest foreign policy issue right now is the U.S. conflict with Iran. According to the Congressional Research Service (CRS), the nonpartisan research office that works for Congress:4

  • February 28, 2026: U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran begin, starting a wider regional conflict. Iran responds with attacks on targets across the Middle East and tries to control the Strait of Hormuz, a key route for the world's oil.
  • March 2: The President formally notifies Congress, starting the War Powers Resolution's 60-day clock.5
  • April: The two sides agree to a ceasefire.
  • May 1: The 60-day mark arrives. The White House argues the war has ended because of the ceasefire, so it does not need approval from Congress.5
  • June: The U.S. and Iran sign a Memorandum of Understanding. On June 23, the Senate passes a war powers resolution 50 to 48 directing the President to remove U.S. forces.6
  • August: The agreement breaks down and expires.
  • September: The U.S. and Iran are again trading limited strikes, and the U.S. increases sanctions. On September 24, another Senate war powers resolution fails 49 to 50.7

U.S. officials have said the goals include destroying Iran's ballistic missile program and navy and making sure Iran never gets a nuclear weapon.4 A September Pentagon inspector general report also found that the war has used up so many munitions that it has caused shortages in U.S. weapons stockpiles.4

Concern #1: Action Without Congress

Congress never voted to authorize the war with Iran. When the 60-day deadline came, members of Congress could not even agree on when the clock had started or whether it still applied.5 The Senate votes in June and September were close, and four Republican senators joined Democrats in September, which shows this is not just a partisan issue.7

This is the exact situation the founders tried to prevent. When one person can start a war and Congress cannot or will not stop it, the system of checks and balances is not working.

Concern #2: The Cost to Americans

Wars are paid for by taxpayers, and they also raise prices on everyday things.

  • Military costs: The Congressional Budget Office estimated the military operation cost $38 billion as of August 1, 2026.4
  • Household costs: Moody's Analytics estimates the war is costing American households about $2,000 each, counting military spending, higher interest rates, and higher prices for gas, groceries, and transportation.8
  • Gas prices: By October, the national average had been at or above $4 a gallon for more than 165 days in 2026, the most of any year since AAA started tracking in 2000.8
  • Defense budget: The White House requested about $1.5 trillion for defense in fiscal year 2027, the largest request ever.25

Every dollar spent on a war without a clear goal is a dollar that does not go to education, health care, infrastructure, or paying down the national debt.

Concern #3: The Public Is Left Out

When wars start without a vote in Congress, there is no big public debate. Most Americans never hear the arguments for and against until troops are already deployed. Members of Congress respond to what their voters tell them, so if the public stays quiet, nothing changes. The public needs to speak up.

Our Solutions

1. Greater congressional oversight

Congress should vote before any major military action, except when the U.S. must respond to a sudden attack. We support strengthening the War Powers Resolution so it is harder to work around, and replacing open-ended authorizations like the 2001 AUMF with ones that expire unless Congress renews them. Congress has shown it can do this: in December 2025 it repealed the 1991 and 2002 Iraq war authorizations.17

2. Diplomacy first

Before force, the U.S. should exhaust negotiations, sanctions, and work with allies. Some of the most successful decisions in U.S. history, like the Marshall Plan and the peaceful handling of the Cuban Missile Crisis, came from diplomacy instead of war.14

3. More transparency to the public

The government should publish clear, regular reports on where U.S. forces are fighting, under what legal authority, and how much each operation costs taxpayers. People cannot hold their leaders accountable for decisions they do not know about.
