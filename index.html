United States Foreign Policy Restriction Administration

Putting national interest before intervention.

USFPRA is a student-led, nonpartisan interest group. We believe the decision to send Americans to war belongs to Congress, made in public and on the record, not to one person acting alone.

Who we are

A student voice on the biggest decision a country can make.

Our mission: USFPRA promotes thoughtful limits, diplomacy, and public accountability in U.S. foreign policy.

Name
United States Foreign Policy Restriction Administration. “Restriction” because our cause is limits: clear rules about when and how force is used. “Administration” because we want those rules carried out.
Slogan
“Putting National Interest Before Intervention.” Before any intervention, someone should have to ask out loud whether it serves the American people.
Type of group
Ideological + public-interest. We advocate for a principle (constitutional checks on war), and the benefit goes to the whole public, not just our members.
Party
None. Presidents of both parties have stretched war powers. Our allies range from progressives to libertarians to veterans' groups.

How we chose this cause

  1. We listed the issues we care about. We kept returning to foreign policy, because it affects spending, safety, and lives more than almost any other issue.
  2. We found a gap. Most groups argue about which war is right. Few ask who decides. That question cuts across party lines.
  3. We saw it happening in 2026. The U.S. went to war with Iran in February without a congressional vote, and Congress spent months arguing over whether it even had a say.
  4. It is personal for our generation. Automatic draft registration for men ages 18–25 is set to start this December. People our age carry the costs of these decisions.
The status quo

The Constitution says Congress decides. History tells a different story.

The founders gave Congress the power to start wars and the President the power to command them. Over 80 years that balance has shifted, a little at a time, through blank-check authorizations and loopholes.

1787

The Constitution splits the power

Article I: Congress declares war and controls funding. Article II: the President is Commander in Chief.

1942

The last declaration of war

Congress formally declares war on Bulgaria, Hungary, and Romania during WWII. It has not declared war since.

1964

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution

It passed 416–0 and 88–2 after a disputed naval incident, and it became the legal basis for escalating the Vietnam War.

1973

War Powers Resolution

Passed over President Nixon's veto. The President must notify Congress within 48 hours and withdraw within 60 days (plus 30) unless Congress approves.

2001

The 60-word blank check

Three days after 9/11, Congress passed an Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) 420–1 and 98–0. Four presidents have since used it to justify operations in many countries. It is still in force 25 years later.

2011

The “hostilities” loophole

During the Libya air campaign, the administration argued that bombing didn't count as “hostilities,” so the 60-day clock never started.

2025

A rare win

Congress repealed the 1991 and 2002 Iraq war authorizations in the defense bill signed December 18. Senator Kaine called it the first repeal of a war authorization in over 50 years.

2026

War with Iran, with no vote

U.S. strikes began February 28 with no authorization. When the 60-day mark arrived, the White House said a ceasefire meant the war was “over.” The Senate passed a war powers resolution 50–48 in June, then rejected another 49–50 in September.

Founding ruleCongress's role weakenedCongress pushed back

The problem isn't one president or one party. The rules have loopholes, and every administration since 1973 has used them.

It's our draft card

Under the FY2026 defense law, the Selective Service is set to begin automatically registering men ages 18–25 by December 2026. There is no draft, but the people a war would call on are our age.

It's our money

Today's war spending is largely borrowed. Our generation will pay the interest, and the trade-off is real: every dollar for open-ended war is a dollar not spent on tuition, health, or infrastructure.

It's what we believe

Chicago Council polling finds that pluralities of Millennials and Gen Z think military approaches are overused, and they strongly support diplomacy and international agreements.

What we're asking Congress to do

Four reforms. One principle: vote before war.

We are not against defense. We are against wars that start without a public vote and never officially end.

01

Vote Before War

No offensive military campaign without a recorded vote in Congress. The only exception is repelling a sudden attack, which the founders always allowed.

The issuePresidents of both parties have launched major operations, from Libya in 2011 to Iran in 2026, without authorization.
How to fix itPass a law that limits the President's unilateral authority to self-defense and imminent threats.
02

Sunset the Blank Checks

Replace the 2001 AUMF with a narrow authorization that names its enemies, names the countries, and expires after two years unless Congress renews it.

The issueA 60-word law from 2001 is still being cited 25 years later against groups that didn't exist when it passed.
How to fix itBuild on the 2025 Iraq repeal. Congress has shown it can do this, so finish the job.
03

Close the War Powers Loopholes

Define “hostilities” to include airstrikes and drone strikes, and cut off funding automatically when the clock runs out. A ceasefire should not reset the clock.

The issueThe 2011 “not hostilities” argument and the 2026 “the war is over” claim both sidestepped the 60-day limit.
How to fix itUse the bipartisan National Security Powers Act (Sens. Murphy, Lee, and Sanders, 2021) as a model.
04

Show the Receipt

Require public, plain-language reports on where U.S. forces are fighting, under what legal authority, and what it costs, including a per-taxpayer “war receipt.”

The issueMost Americans can't name the countries where U.S. forces are in combat, or what it costs them.
How to fix itRequire public reports every quarter, with diplomacy-first options considered before force.

Hard questions

Good questions we're ready to answer.

Doesn't this make America look weak?

No. A war that Congress has voted for has the country's backing, and allies and enemies can see that. Wars started by one person can be ended by the next. Public debate makes commitments more believable, not less.

What about emergencies? Congress is slow.

Ask #1 keeps the President's power to repel sudden attacks. That exception has existed since 1787. Congress has also moved fast when it needed to: it passed the 2001 AUMF in three days.

Isn't this just isolationism?

No. We support alliances, trade, and diplomacy. Polling shows young Americans want more international cooperation, not less. Restraint is about how the U.S. uses force, not whether it engages with the world.

Is this anti-Trump or anti-Democrat?

Neither. Obama used the “not hostilities” argument in Libya. Trump launched the 2026 Iran war without a vote. Both parties have used the 2001 AUMF. Our scorecard grades every member the same way.

Why should Congress listen to students?

Because we vote, and our generation will soon be the largest group of voters. Congressional offices track constituent calls. Organized, informed young voters who show up are hard for a member of Congress to ignore.

Putting national interest before intervention means one thing: before America goes to war, America decides. Join us.
